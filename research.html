Latent Statecraft: Political Alignment in the Shadow of Tariffs

with Mostafa Beshkar

The terms-of-trade concessions exchanged under the GATT/WTO are far from reciprocal: the United States concedes substantially more than it receives from many of its partners. We examine whether the United States was compensated for these net concessions in political rather than economic terms, a form of leverage we call latent statecraft. Measuring concessions as the terms-of-trade value of bilateral tariff cuts, which is revalued as trade patterns shift even when tariffs do not change, we find that among transactional partners, larger concessions are associated with closer alignment with the United States in UN General Assembly voting. No such relationship appears among security allies, and the response is concentrated in votes the United States does not designate as important.

Rebalancing Supply Networks after a Geopolitical Shock

solo-authored

This paper asks how sourcing adjusts when an established supplier becomes politically unreliable, using the 2019 Korea–Japan trade dispute. Using quarterly product-level import data, I show that products with more concentrated alternative supply outside Japan saw a larger decline in imports from Japan, but this decline was not offset along the extensive margin: these products added no more supplier countries. Adjustment instead occurred along the intensive margin, as their non-Japanese sourcing became relatively less concentrated across pre-existing suppliers. After the dispute, imports also shifted away from suppliers whose own geopolitical risk rose. Methodologically, I show that classifying products by a country's own pre-shock import concentration generates mean reversion that can be mistaken for rebalancing, and avoid it by measuring concentration from world trade flows that exclude the importer.

Trade Imbalances and Reciprocity in the WTO: A Rule-Based Adjustment Framework

with Mostafa Beshkar

The WTO lacks a mechanism for adjusting tariff commitments as economic conditions change, and the balance of concessions has drifted far from reciprocity, largely because of shifting trade imbalances. We quantify the tariff changes that would restore the balance of concessions between the United States and each partner to its level at the founding of the WTO, and find that partners with which the United States runs larger deficits require systematically larger adjustments. Building on Keynes's principle of symmetric adjustment, we propose a framework that sequences targeted tariff reductions by surplus countries, fees on large deviations from benchmark trade balances, and tariff increases by deficit countries only as a last resort.
